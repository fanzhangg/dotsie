Choosing spots: rules or a model

The spots data isn’t filtered. To choose the places worth a trip from it, the first pilots used rules; the guide now asks a model.

How they compare

The same

  • Both start from the same data: Wikidata items and NPS pages, with English Wikipedia’s articles and page views.
  • Both are checked against the same lists: Wikivoyage’s sights, and the places Seattle must have and must never have.

What’s different

RulesA model
Is it a sight?Wikidata types to keep and to block, and Wikipedia categories for what’s goneSonnet reads each place’s description, article intro, categories, page views and what’s nearby, and says whether it belongs in the guide
Page viewsDecide the tier: a floor per typeOne more fact the model is shown; they decide nothing
The resultTwo tiers: in Washington, 21 places in tier 1 and 347 in tier 2Sonnet ranks every place judged to belong, with a reason each: no cap, no shortlist, as many as the model finds
A mistakeAnother rule or exception, which can break another placeA better prompt, asked again of every place
CostNothing, and the same answer every timeEvery candidate is asked of Sonnet; answers are kept, so a rerun gives the same ones

Accuracy

Seattle, each list’s share of places that Wikivoyage lists as sights (a “see” listing with a Wikidata item, 74 of them). The same check the rules were measured by: theirs in the pilots, the model’s on the live guide in November 2026, when it still picked up to 20 from a shortlist of 70. pipeline/eval_guides.py measures every guide this way.

Model: also counting Wikivoyage’s other listings and district pages89% (17)
  • On the same check, about as good as the best rules. 63% against 60% for a top 20 by languages and views, which had only two months of page views. Tier 1’s 80% is a handful of the most famous places.
  • Wikivoyage is a lower bound. It lists Pike Place Market under Buy, the Underground tour under Do, and Capitol Hill, Fremont and the International District as districts of their own. Counting those, 17 of the 19 picks. The two it doesn’t have: Alki Point (it lists the lighthouse and the beach there) and the University of Washington’s campus.
  • The mistakes are of a different kind. The rules’ were errors: Bill Gates’s house and the Bluetooth SIG ranked near the top, and an urban legend and a bridge that fell in 1940 made tier 1. The model’s two misses are judgment calls. It picked none of the 7 places Seattle must never have, and 14 of the 37 it must have, with room for 20.
  • Not compared: how much each finds. The rules kept hundreds of places (61% of Wikivoyage’s sights); the guide then kept at most 20. It now keeps every place the model says belongs.
  • Why the model answers yes or no. Asked for star ratings, Haiku agreed with Opus 50 to 86% of the time; on “is it a sight?”, 90%. So it isn’t asked to rate: it says whether a place belongs in the guide, and then ranks those that do against each other.
  • No expectations built in. Each rule added to a prompt overreached in the next run (glaciers dropped as “what the park is made of”, Badlands’ trails as trailheads). The code now only decides what a destination is and which records are one place; how many places it has, and which, is the model’s.
